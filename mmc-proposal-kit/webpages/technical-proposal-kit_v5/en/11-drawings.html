How to Read Architectural, Structural and MEP Drawings

A working sequence for reading a drawing set, the symbols that matter, and the details where quantity errors are actually made.

Sections8
Disciplines3
Worked example1
Rev.A
The rule that prevents most take-off errors: read the sheet, then the schedule, then the detail, then the specification, then measure. Each of those five sources can contradict the one before it, and the contradiction is where the money is lost. A dimension scaled from a PDF is not a dimension.

1 Before You Read a Single Line

  1. Read the drawing register and the revision history

    The register tells you what should exist; the revision block tells you what changed. A take-off from a superseded revision is the most expensive mistake in estimating, and it is silent — the sheet looks exactly like the current one.

  2. Confirm the revision you are measuring is the current issued revision

    Check the revision letter on the sheet against the transmittal register, not against the file name. Files are renamed; drawings are not.

  3. Establish the scale and the units, and then do not scale

    Most sets are in millimetres; some structural details are in centimetres. Measure only from the written dimensions; use the scale bar to check that a printed copy is not distorted.

  4. Identify the grid and the datum before anything else

    Grid lines give you coordinates for every element; the datum gives you a single reference for every level. Without both, a slab thickness becomes a guess.

  5. Read the general notes

    Concrete grades, cover, blockwork strengths, plaster thickness and paint systems are frequently stated only here — and they govern the whole take-off.

2 Architectural Drawings

Architectural drawings answer what the building is: spaces, dimensions, levels, finishes and openings. They are the source for blockwork, plaster, tiling, doors and windows — but only after the finishes schedule has been cross-checked, because room finishes are rarely written on the plan.

Where architectural take-off goes wrong

Openings deducted twice, or not at all. Establish one rule — deduct openings above a stated area — and apply it to every wall, every time. A mixed approach produces a quantity nobody can reconcile.
Wall height assumed from floor-to-floor. Blockwork stops at the soffit; the beam depth is not blockwork. Measure the actual clear height per wall.
Finishes measured from the schedule instead of the plan. The schedule tells you what; only the plan tells you how much.

3 Structural Drawings

Structural drawings answer what carries the load and how. They are the source for concrete, reinforcement, formwork and structural steel — and the discipline where a misread symbol costs the most, because reinforcement is priced by weight and weight comes from bar diameter and length.

Reading a reinforcement drawing in the right order

  1. Element, then mark: identify the element (footing F1, column C2, beam B12) and its mark. Everything else references the mark.
  2. Section or schedule: read the bar arrangement from the section, and the bar mark, diameter, shape and bending dimensions from the schedule.
  3. Cutting length: from the bending schedule — including hooks, bends and laps. This is the only correct input for weight; a scaled length from the drawing omits every lap.
  4. Weight: $$\text{kg/m} = \frac{d^2}{162}$$ for deformed bar — 12 mm gives 0.888 kg/m, 16 mm gives 1.578 kg/m, 20 mm gives 2.469 kg/m, 25 mm gives 3.854 kg/m.
  5. Links: count along the member at the specified spacing, add the end zones where spacing is tightened, and cut to the perimeter less cover plus hooks.
  6. Laps and anchorage: add them. A take-off that ignores laps under-measures reinforcement by roughly 3–8% depending on bar size and detailing.

4 MEP Drawings

MEP drawings answer how the building services work and where they run. Read them as systems, not as lines: a pipe on a plan means nothing until you know what it carries, what pressure it works at, and where it rises.

The services take-off rule

Measure pipe and duct by centre-line length, then add fittings separately. Never measure the drawn length of a pipe that turns: the drawing shows the run, the fittings schedule gives the elbows. A pipe take-off without fittings is typically 30–40% short of the installed cost.

Why the reinforcement figure is 11.248 t and not a percentage: a percentage-of-concrete-volume estimate would give somewhere between 100 and 140 kg/m³, i.e. 10.3–14.5 t on 102.7 m³ — a range of ±20%. The schedule total is exact and defensible. Use the percentage only as a sanity check on the schedule, never as the quantity.

7 Documenting Your Reading

Every take-off must be traceable back to a sheet, a revision and a dimension. Record, for each measured item: drawing number, revision, the element mark, and the dimensions used. When the drawing is revised, you can then identify exactly which quantities change — and claim the difference if it is a variation.
